###  Unraveling Failure - Lessons from an Avoidable Ransomware Attack

A real-world ransomware attack on a non-IT company where cybersecurity wasn’t a priority. Learn how incident management and business continuity collapsed under pressure, what really happens during an attack, and the lessons leaders must learn shared from real cases presented at Bsides

----------

Ransomware is no longer an abstract IT risk is an operational crisis. This talk presents a real-life ransomware attack against a large, non-IT industrial company where cybersecurity was not considered a business priority.

Through a chronological breakdown of the incident, we explore how a single phishing email escalated into a full IT blackout, shutting down operations, disrupting production, and paralyzing the business for months. The session focuses on incident management under pressure and the failure and rebuilding of the Business Continuity Plan.

Attendees will gain an inside view of:

What actually happens during a ransomware attack, beyond theory and frameworks  
How organizational mindset and management decisions amplify impact  
Why missing “basic” security controls turns incidents into disasters  
Practical lessons learned during recovery and transformation  
This talk is based on a real case, previously presented at BSides Chișinău and BSides Cluj(you can have feedback from the organizers if needed), and is aimed at both technical and non-technical audiences who want to understand ransomware from a business-impact perspective not just a technical one.

Analysis of rganizational maturity in terms of cybersecurity. 

Real world example of working with the IT infrastructure of a large company with 42000 emplotees and 3M USD budget for cyber. 120 IT personel in total with 7 dedicated to cybersec. Large industrial company that is not in IT. 

Speaker was a cybersecurity consultant working on developing a policy for cybersecurity as they were facing a high cybersecurity risk and were dangerously close to a point of no recovery in the event of a cyberattack. They created their first cybersecurity policy taking another companies policy and changing the name of the company. They had no anti-virus solution for hardware, only some solutions for email security. The company was resistend to things like windows updates, and putting security applications on the personal devices of the CEO. 

Structure of the typically non-IT company is to have the CISO directly under the CTO who is directly under the CEO, which means IT is manages in the same way as the rest of the buisness. IT was not seen as essential and was treated as if it might have been something like marketing. 

CyberSec indicators showed a huge dbt, 250K+ vulnerabilities. 12% successful phishing testing. 20% response rate to security training. No collaboration between IT and the HR department. Accounts were kept active after people were leaving the company, which was often fast because of the high staff turnover rate.

When presented with these issues the C-suite said that they agreed something needed to be done but that now was not the right time to do it. This lead to an IT blackout in March 2023 where access cards, credentials and applications were not working. The backups had been deleted and could not be accessed, with an offer to give the data back for the price of 1.5 million dollars. 

The beginnig of the ransomeware attack was a phishing attack that lead to credential theft leading to vulnerabilities exploitation as well as lateral movement into ransomeware spreading. They reached the backups and deleted everything. There was an telephone service related to the phishing attack. 

These types of attacks are very easy to do, ransome ware as a service applications on the dark web, exploit detection can be performed automatically and this can mean as soon as vulnerabilities exist, bots can be searching for them. This attack was done by [Black Basta](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/black-basta-exposed-leaked-dive-ransomware-empire-its-bertrand-9x02e/)

**Notable Victims and Financial Impact**

Black Basta's operations have affected a diverse range of sectors, including healthcare, manufacturing, and finance. Notable incidents include attacks on Capita, ABB, and Dish Network. Collectively, victims have paid over $100 million in ransoms, with the group executing more than 329 attacks since its inception.

**Recent Developments**

In February 2025, internal chat logs from Black Basta were leaked online, providing unprecedented insight into the group's internal communications and operational strategies. The leak, potentially resulting from internal disputes or dissatisfaction among affiliates, offers valuable information for cybersecurity professionals aiming to understand and combat the group's methodologies.

**Conclusion**

Black Basta exemplifies the evolving nature of ransomware threats, characterized by sophisticated techniques and substantial financial extortion. The recent exposure of their internal communications underscores the importance of continuous monitoring, information sharing, and proactive defense measures to mitigate the impact of such cyber threats.

This specific attack took 4 days from phishing to a locked system with ransomeware request from the attackers. At first they didn't have a backup even though there was a different company doing the IT security. The external company noticed the deleting of the backups and were able to recover some of the information from the backups. On March 20th there was the phishing attack, on the 24th there was the ransomeware. This ended up with a cost of above 1 million dollars per day. A lot of the internal blame in the company was placed on the IT deparment. 

During the crisi management, all operations started to be moved onto paper organization, the entire IT deparment was working >12/hr days. After 7 days the countermeasure begam focusing on backup storage. The company chose not to pay and bought all new devices for networking, laptops, etc. And they were moving their laptops and other IT devices using their company airplane instead of shipping it through a third party delivery service. For all of the old devices, they were treated as being 'red' initially and then slowly moved to being 'orange' until they were secure enough to be added back onto the network. 

Then between the crisis management and countermeasure starting, the attackers contacted them because they hadn't heard from them. Typically the cost that is decided by the attackers is low enough that it makes sense to just pay the ransom. They are working at a company like organization. This company didn't have any IT related insurance to pay for these types of issues. 

During the incidant +20 days range, there was too much pressure on people in the company coming from management, and people started to leave the company. A 'war room' was set up to manage the crisis to have different people working together easily. But they fired the CISO during this attack, making it difficult to organize the crisis efficiently. 6 months after the incident there was full service restoration and 10 million USD was invested into IT and cybersecurity. This included a change in the mindset in the management towards cybersecurity, but the change was somewhat short lived and they started sending their assistents to meetings. 

Some of the mistakes were treating sec as secondary, and the lesson learned was to treat a cybersecurity attack as immenent. A mistake was missing minimum security measures, and the lesson learned was that xyberseec basics needed ot be covered. There was a lack of security monitoring which taught them to have regular security audits. During the event there were managemnt changes during the crisis and tensions, which taught the lesson not to change management in a crisis. There were communication deficits between different departments which alloweed them to learn the necessity of establishign crisis communication plans. There was long implementation times with internal resources which taught them that they need to add extenral partners in crisis to acclerate restoration. High pressure on the employees taguht people to be more calm during issues that were taking place.

LLMs are just one piece of the risk, and one piece of the attack surface of an agent. This talk will focus on identifying attack surface components, considering them as a whole, and discussing both defending and attacking agents.

----------

The industry's current focus on Large Language Model (LLM) security is largely obsessed with the "model-as-a-target" (e.g., prompt injection and jailbreaking). However, we've now seen 100s of real-world examples of breaches, exploits and data leakages, and only a percentage of them are attributable to pure LLM manipulation. As organizations transition from simple chatbots to autonomous AI Agents—capable of executing code, calling APIs, and managing long-term memory—the attack surface expands far beyond the model itself. Securing these systems requires a fundamental shift toward a Full-Stack Agentic Risk Assessment approach.

Key Takeaways:

-   A framework for thinking about an agent's architecture and consolidated attack surface.
-   A "Top 10" checklist for assessing the security posture of an autonomous agent.
-   Strategies for validating agent outputs and enforcing range/type constraints during cross-system handoffs.
-   How to incorporate model-centric security to a composite, end-to-end risk assessment.
